Advances in Wearable Thermoelectric Devices: Strategies for Enhancing Wearability and Performance

Yurim Lee†, Hyein Ham†, Siyeon Park, Hanbit Jang, Seungwan Hong, Jungmok Seo*

Small Science, 6(9), e70402 (2026)

Jungmok Seo, who now leads the BLISS Lab in the School of Electrical and Electronic Engineering at Yonsei University, is the corresponding author of this paper.

DOI: 10.1002/smsc.70402 ↗

Why this research matters

This review examines design strategies that balance wearability with thermoelectric power generation and cooling. It considers how skin contact, deformable electrical connections and heat-transfer paths constrain device-level performance.

The problem

Wearable thermoelectric devices must move with the body while harvesting or transferring heat. Skin contact, electrical connections and heat rejection can limit performance even when the thermoelectric material works well. The review follows these links from material properties to the assembled wearable.

How the study approaches it

A compliant structure can improve contact with skin, but greater flexibility alone does not ensure better thermoelectric performance. Architecture changes both electrical and thermal paths. Material choice, component arrangement and stable contact therefore need to be designed together.

What was tested

Power generation depends on the temperature difference across the device and its electrical load. Cooling depends on input power, heat removal from the hot side and skin contact. These boundary conditions need separate treatment; electrical output and cooling temperature do not form one common ranking.

What the study found

The review describes a pop-up kirigami device that kept contact with a cylindrical heat source and maintained similar output during stretching. Its geometry accommodated movement without stretching the thermoelectric material itself. The cited test used a 100 °C source, not body-temperature heat harvesting, and the original raw data were not independently reviewed.

What remains to be answered

Repeated wear raises questions about contact and connection stability. Comparisons need matched area and ambient conditions, supported by the original device protocols. The selected review sections do not establish long-term safety or an architecture that is best for every use.

How it connects to other research

The related modular, self-healing thermoelectric device provides a concrete architecture for generation, cooling and heating. Its abstract helps compare the scope of the designs. Self-healing alone does not establish superior efficiency or cooling, and output under an imposed temperature difference is not everyday wearable output.
