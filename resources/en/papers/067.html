Lubricant skin on diverse biomaterials with complex shapes via polydopamine-mediated surface functionalization for biomedical applications

Kijun Park, Seunghoi Kim, Yejin Jo, Jae Park, Inwoo Kim, Sooyoung Hwang, Yeontaek Lee, Soyeon Kim, Jungmok Seo*

Bioactive Materials, 25, 555-568 (2023)

DOI: 10.1016/j.bioactmat.2022.07.019 ↗

Research context and placement

The read text covers lubricated antifouling coatings for complex devices, including device, cell and ex vivo blood tests. A coating process does not establish electrical interconnect–component integration, supporting the host–material interface category.

Research background

Complex medical devices require both coating attachment and a lubricated interface on narrow passages and curved surfaces. This study focuses on forming such interfaces across diverse device surfaces. Its fabrication approach and surface tests must be distinguished from evidence for long-term use or clinical efficacy.

Approach and advances

A PDA bioadhesive layer anchors carboxyl-functional fluoropolymer PFP, which is swollen with PFPE lubricant to form L-skin. The main text covers pipette tips, bioprinting nozzles, PDMS microfluidic channels, tubing and Foley catheters. The contribution is a fabrication route for diverse materials and complex geometries; equivalent lifetime and safety across all medical devices are not established.

Evaluation and conditions

Wetting, sliding and device demonstrations must be separated from protein adsorption, NIH3T3 fibroblast and RAW264.7 macrophage adhesion, extract cytotoxicity, and ex vivo horse-blood clotting and platelet observations. Contact and sliding angles after sterilization treatments assess retained surface behavior; they do not directly establish microbial killing or a validated sterility-assurance level.

Key findings

The main text reports reduced protein adsorption and adhesion in the two cell models. The noncytotoxicity result from the extract assay using fibroblasts applies only to that assay, while clot-weight and platelet observations apply to ex vivo horse blood. Neither establishes general human safety. Thermal recovery must be linked to Methods §4.14: at least four hours of lubricant swelling followed by treatment at 100°C, rather than spontaneous recovery at body temperature.

Limits and open questions

§3 explicitly identifies an unmet need for in vivo validation. Sterilization treatments require method-specific conditions; detailed outsourced EO parameters are not stated in the main text. Comprehensive conclusions about lubricant retention, use-related damage and long-term biological responses remain pending. Unread SI and internal discrepancies in flow, time, cross-cut testing, bioink and dimensions preclude selecting definitive numerical effects or a single resolved protocol.

Connections to related work

The existing external SLIPS comparator remains abstract-only. Its lubricated-interface concept can be related to L-skin fabrication on complex devices, without ranking durability, recovery, sterilization compatibility or blood compatibility. A direct comparison requires additional source reading with matched geometry, lubricant retention and loss, treatment conditions and biological models.
