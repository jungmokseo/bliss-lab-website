Lubricant‐Coated Organ‐on‐a‐Chip for Enhanced Precision in Preclinical Drug Testing

Tae Young Kim, Jeong-Won Choi, Kijun Park, SeungHwan Kim, Jeong F. Kim, Tae-Eun Park*, Jungmok Seo*

Small, 20(43), 2402431 (2024)

DOI: 10.1002/smll.202402431 ↗

Why this research matters

This in-vitro organ-on-a-chip uses lubricated channel walls to reduce drug loss while preserving cell attachment on a separate culture membrane. Intestinal and blood–brain-barrier models examine how the surface design affects drug exposure and biological readouts.

Drug loss and cell-culture interfaces in organ-on-a-chip models

PDMS can absorb small molecules from a microfluidic channel, changing the drug dose that reaches cultured cells. Yet cells need a surface they can attach to. This study gives drug-contacting channel walls and the cell-supporting membrane different surface functions.

How the study approaches it

Lubricated channel walls reduce nonspecific drug loss, while an extracellular-matrix (ECM) coating on a semiporous PET membrane supports cells. Separating these regions allows the chip to resist unwanted attachment where drugs flow and support needed attachment where cells grow.

What was tested

Fluorescence and drug-recovery tests in empty chips measure loss along the fluid pathway. Gut-toxicity and blood–brain-barrier transport tests assess biological responses in cell-containing chips. Better drug recovery does not by itself establish clinical prediction by those models.

What the study found

In the gut-model DEX concentration series, PreD showed a lower IC50 than the conventional chip under matched flow and exposure. IC50 is the concentration associated with a half-maximal inhibitory response. The result connects reduced device loss with a changed toxicity readout for DEX and Caco-2 cells, rather than validating clinical toxicity predictions for all drugs.

What remains to be answered

Comparisons require matched molecular properties, concentration, flow, exposure and cell/membrane conditions. Coating durability and recovery of a wider range of drugs need further testing. The authors also call for validation of clinical predictive value, so this is not an established replacement for clinical testing.

How it connects to other research

Toepke and Beebe describe the background problem of small molecules partitioning into PDMS. PreD addresses that problem through different surface designs for different chip regions. The external abstract supports the problem's relevance, not equal coating benefits or validated biological responses for every molecule.
