Mechanically Robust and Anti‐Biofouling Hybrid Encapsulation via Layered Organic–Liquid Interfaces for Implantable Devices

Sangwoo Park†, Kijun Park†, Tae Young Kim, Yejin Jo, Jangho Kim, Kayoung Son, Hayoung Choi, Myoung-Ryul Ok, Su Ryon Shin*, Jungmok Seo*

Small, 22(17), e10949 (2026)

Jungmok Seo, who now leads the BLISS Lab in the School of Electrical and Electronic Engineering at Yonsei University, is the corresponding author of this paper.

DOI: 10.1002/smll.202510949 ↗

Why this research matters

Bonded silicone and Parylene C layers protect implantable devices against water and ion ingress, while an outer lubricated interface addresses biofouling. The study evaluates electrical isolation in buffer and tissue responses after seven days of subcutaneous implantation.

Protective encapsulation and biofouling in soft implants

An implant's soft encapsulation must move with it while limiting water and ion entry. Stiff barriers may fracture, while compliant polymers can remain permeable. MOLE assigns protective and antifouling functions to bonded layers and an outer lubricated interface.

How the study approaches it

Amine-functionalized silicone and Parylene C form bonded layers intended to resist separation and ingress. Oil infused into the outer silicone changes the surface in contact with tissue. Controlling biological attachment at the surface and protecting electrical components within the package require separate evidence.

What was tested

Mechanical tests assess separation between layers; leakage-current and impedance tests in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) assess electrical isolation. Fouling assays and subcutaneous implantation examine biological interaction. The magnesium antenna's S11 measurement tracks a moisture-sensitive component, not the lifetime of every possible implant.

What the study found

In the PBS tests, MOLE maintained high impedance and low leakage longer than the Parylene C comparator. This supports delayed formation of conductive paths through the encapsulation. Impedance change and leakage failure still use different criteria, while protein and tissue tests provide separate evidence about the biological interface.

What remains to be answered

Seven days of implantation cannot establish chronic tissue response or long-term lubricant retention. Converting accelerated-temperature results into a physiological lifetime also requires an appropriate failure model. Repeated deformation, oil retention and longer-term safety remain open questions.

How it connects to other research

Ultraflexible organic photonic skin provides a related example of protection in thin, compliant electronics. MOLE adds questions about implanted water/ion ingress and tissue interaction. The external abstract supports this design connection, without ranking barrier lifetimes or electrical performance across on-skin and implanted devices.
