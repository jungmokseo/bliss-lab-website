Functional Bioelectronic Materials for Long-Term Biocompatibility and Functionality

Jae Park†, Yeontaek Lee†, Tae Young Kim, Sooyoung Hwang, Jungmok Seo*

ACS Applied Electronic Materials, 4(4), 1449-1468 (2022)

DOI: 10.1021/acsaelm.1c01212 ↗

The question behind this study

This review examines materials that help bioelectronics keep functioning amid body motion, biofluids and surrounding cellular responses. It connects flexibility, antifouling, encapsulation, adhesion and self-healing with mechanical, biological and interfacial compatibility.

Conditions for lasting bioelectronic function

Wearable and implantable bioelectronics need stable tissue integration while recording signals or delivering therapeutic stimulation. Mechanical mismatch, protein/immune-cell interactions and foreign-body responses, and moist moving interfaces each challenge continued function. The review separates these into physical, biological and interfacial compatibility. Skin attachment or short-term signal detection alone does not establish long-term stability and biocompatibility inside tissue.

Physical, biological and interfacial design

Physical design distinguishes flexibility obtained by thinning from geometric or intrinsic stretchability. Biological strategies include PEG, zwitterionic and lubricant antifouling interfaces, degradable materials, local drug delivery and immobilized biomolecules. Interfacial strategies cover organic/inorganic biofluid barriers, bioinspired adhesives, and healing insulators, conductors and semiconductors. The contribution connects these functions to different causes of failure during prolonged use; it does not mean that every strategy has been validated together in one device.

Separating deformation, signals and lifetime

This is a review of heterogeneous device, material and tissue studies, rather than a new shared cohort. Device and local-material strain in Figure 3, signals/antifouling in Figure 4, degradation/drug transport in Figure 5, and adhesion, peeling, resistance and damage recovery in Figures 6–7 are separate endpoints. Table 1 distinguishes encapsulation material/thickness, test temperature, measured results and some projected lifetimes at 37°C. Compare solution, temperature, failure criterion, acceleration model and actual in vivo follow-up together. Bending or restored appearance alone is not common evidence of prolonged electrical operation.

Materials chosen for specific failure paths

The reviewed examples present routes for thin/stretchable structures to accommodate deformation, antifouling or local bioactivity to modulate interface responses, and encapsulation/adhesion to reduce deterioration from fluids and movement. Organic barriers provide flexibility and dense inorganic layers provide blocking ability, but permeability and mechanical damage motivate hybrid structures. Healing examples also use different conditions for mechanical continuity and recovery of resistance or charge transport. This synthesis identifies design options for specific failure paths rather than establishing a universal implant lifetime or patient-level benefit.

Projected lifetime versus observed operation

Thinning can improve bending flexibility without providing large tensile stretchability, and overall-device strain differs from local material strain. Antifouling, degradation and release need assessment of persistence, byproducts, location and dose. Barrier lifetimes projected from heated solutions are not observed implantation years; semiconductor repair requiring heat or solvent is not autonomous physiological healing. Implantation burden, multisignal integration and wireless or battery-free operation remain challenges. Different models and durations, with supplementary information, raw data and cited originals outside this account’s scope, prevent conclusions about clinical safety or superiority.

Linking foreign-body responses to device design

The related abstract of “Foreign body reaction to biomaterials” (DOI 10.1016/j.smim.2007.11.004) links surface properties with macrophage and foreign-body giant-cell responses. This review connects that biology with mechanical matching, fluid barriers, adhesion and healing in device design. The external full text is outside the comparison’s scope. Individual coatings need evidence from their own devices, tissues and follow-up conditions.
