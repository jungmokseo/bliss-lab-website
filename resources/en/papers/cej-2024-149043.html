Bioactivated lubricant-infused surfaces: A dual-action strategy for enhancing osseointegration and preventing implant-associated infections

Jae Park, Daun Jeong, Yeontaek Lee, Kijun Park, Tae Young Kim, Ji Hye Choi, Woo Young Jang*, Jungmok Seo*

Chemical Engineering Journal, 482, 149043 (2024)

Jungmok Seo, who now leads the BLISS Lab in the School of Electrical and Electronic Engineering at Yonsei University, is the corresponding author of this paper.

DOI: 10.1016/j.cej.2024.149043 ↗

Why this research matters

Titanium bearing immobilized bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP-2) and a lubricated interface is designed to resist fouling while supporting osteoblast responses. Bone formation and contact in a rabbit infection model were evaluated separately from pull-out strength in additional, noninoculated animals.

The problem

An implant that contacts bone needs to resist fouling while supporting osteoblast attachment and bone formation. This study asks whether a lubricated surface can meet both needs. Fouling control and integration with bone remain distinct outcomes of the same surface design.

How the study approaches it

BILS combines alkali-etched titanium, partial TPFS fluorination, APTES amine functionalization, immobilized BMP-2 and PFPE lubricant infusion. Under the tested conditions, osteoblasts attached and spread, while fibroblasts that adhered weakly remained rounded without spreading. The design aims to retain LOIS's antifouling function while supporting bone-related cells; it does not capture every cell type or eliminate all fibroblast adhesion.

What was tested

Cell tests measured attachment and spreading, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) at days 7 and 14, and calcium at days 21 and 28. The bone study randomized 16 six-month-old male New Zealand white rabbits among uninfected bare, infected bare, LOIS and BILS groups. Except for the uninfected controls, implants were exposed to MRSA for 12 hours before implantation; X-ray, micro-CT and histology were assessed at day 28. Pull-out tests used three additional rabbits, one each for bare, LOIS and BILS, without bacterial exposure.

What the study found

Cell attachment, spreading and differentiation-related measurements help assess selectivity for osteoblasts. Bone volume and bone–implant contact supported the authors' report of better integration with BILS than LOIS. In the separate, noninoculated pull-out experiment, BILS exceeded LOIS but remained below uninfected bare titanium. This does not establish attachment strength after infection or complete tissue osseointegration in any group.

What remains to be answered

The infection study in Methods §2.12 and pull-out tests in §2.15 used different animals and conditions. Although discussed together in §3.6, they cannot show that attachment strength was validated after infection. Pull-out testing used only one additional rabbit per group, so specimen counts must not be presented as animal counts. The 28-day model, lack of demonstrated complete osseointegration and unread supplementary information and raw data leave human efficacy and long-term safety unresolved.

How it connects to other research

The LOIS control directly tests the balance between a lubricated antifouling surface and osteoblast response. The gentamicin/BMP-2 titanium study (DOI 10.1016/j.bone.2012.01.007) offers an abstract-level example of assessing antibacterial and bone-integration goals together. Its original article and supplements were not reread; differences in materials, infection design and animal conditions prevent direct performance ranking or claims of clinical superiority.
