Modulus-tunable multifunctional hydrogel ink with nanofillers for 3D-Printed soft electronics

Minkyong Kang, Jae Park, Soo A Kim, Tae Young Kim, Ju Yeon Kim, Dae Woo Kim, Kijun Park*, Jungmok Seo*

Biosensors & Bioelectronics, 255, 116257 (2024)

DOI: 10.1016/j.bios.2024.116257 ↗

Why this research matters

This study uses nanoclay and carbon nanotubes to tune hydrogel-ink printability, stiffness and conductivity. It develops inks with distinct structural and conductive roles for 3D-printed forms and stretchable sensors.

Hydrogel inks for 3D-printed soft electronics

A hydrogel for printed soft electronics must flow during printing, hold its shape afterward and move with the skin. Sensing also requires a conductive path. THIN uses different additions to a shared polymer network to adjust these functions for different tasks.

How the study approaches it

THIN-X adds XLG nanoclay to a PVA/tannic-acid/PAA network to adjust physical interactions, mechanics and shape retention. THIN-C adds functionalized carbon nanotubes to provide conduction, while also changing mechanical behavior. A filler that helps one property may not improve conductivity, strength and stretchability together.

What was tested

Flow tests assess extrusion and shape retention; mechanical tests assess deformation; resistance measurements assess sensing. Porcine-skin adhesion is evaluated separately. Results obtained with additional NHS functionalization should not be attributed to every base formulation.

What the study found

After cut THIN-X was rejoined for five minutes, fracture stress and the stress–strain response were reported to recover to their initial levels. Failure away from the joined interface further supported recovery through dynamic bonds. This result concerns the material's mechanics, not simultaneous repair of wiring, skin adhesion or a complete device.

What remains to be answered

Comparisons require matched nozzle and print-path settings, hydration, specimen dimensions and electrode layout. Water loss and changes in attachment during repeated use remain open questions. The inspected tests do not establish long-term human use or clinical-grade sensing.

The text and figures differ in the swelling calculation, conductivity change and impedance units. These values require attention to their measurement conditions and displayed units rather than consolidation into a single performance claim.

How it connects to other research

Yuk and colleagues print a conducting PEDOT:PSS network, while THIN-C uses a CNT-filled hydrogel. Their printing and device sections offer a useful comparison of how conductive paths are built. Different hydration and geometries prevent simple rankings by conductivity or maximum strain.
