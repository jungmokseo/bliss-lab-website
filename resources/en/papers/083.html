Rapid and Scalable Lubrication Coating for Industrial and Medical Applications via Sequential Dip‐Coating

Yeontaek Lee†, Kayoung Son†, Yejin Jo, Seung Hyun Lee, Jeuhee Lee, Tae Young Kim, Seonghyeon Eom, Youn-Hoo Hwang, Dae-Eun Kim, Inhee Choi, Jung Seung Lee, Jungmok Seo*

Advanced Materials Interfaces, 12(14), e00353 (2025)

Jungmok Seo, who now leads the BLISS Lab in the School of Electrical and Electronic Engineering at Yonsei University, is the corresponding author of this paper.

DOI: 10.1002/admi.202500353 ↗

Why this research matters

Two sequential dip-coating steps create an adsorbed base and a mobile lubricant layer. The study combines perfluoropolyether liquids of different molecular weights to reduce friction and fouling, then tests surface function after abrasion and deformation.

The problem

Abrasion and bending can disrupt a lubricated surface. SLIME separates an adsorbed base from a mobile lubricant layer to address this problem. Keeping a coating attached and redistributing liquid after damage are related, but different, functions.

How the study approaches it

Lower-molecular-weight PFPE adsorbs to the substrate after solvent evaporation. Higher-molecular-weight PFPE then forms a compatible mobile liquid layer. Recovery relies on liquid redistribution at the interface rather than reconstruction of a cured solid network.

What was tested

Chemistry, thickness, roughness and transparency describe the coating before use. Abrasion and deformation tests examine whether it keeps functioning afterward. Fouling assays are needed separately to establish how those surface properties affect biological attachment.

What the study found

After swab abrasion, droplets still rolled without pinning on SLIME; the main text reports pinning on the self-assembled-monolayer (SAM) comparator. The result links retained function to redistribution of mobile PFPE. The supplementary evidence was not fully reviewed, and redistribution does not imply unlimited recovery after lubricant is lost.

What remains to be answered

Further comparisons need matched damage severity and recovery time, as well as measurements of lubricant retention under flow. Substrate material and geometry may affect the outcome. The inspected biocompatibility tests do not establish long-term implantation safety.

How it connects to other research

SLIPS retains lubricant within a porous structure, whereas SLIME uses an adsorption-based approach. Their abstracts support comparing how liquid is retained. Pressure stability and recovery after abrasion remain different durability tests.
