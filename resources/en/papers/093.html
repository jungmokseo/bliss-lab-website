PUBLICATION 093 / 2026Summary based on the main text

Quinone-mediated, tissue-adaptive double-network hydrogel for instant hemostasis and wet-tissue adhesion

Tae Young Kim†, Kayoung Son†, Chang-Hwan Moon, Keun-Young Yook, Soo A Kim, Hyein Ham, Yurim Lee, Soo In Lee, Yejin Jo, Yunlong Yu, Dae-Hyun Kim, Jungmok Seo*

Nature Communications, 17, 5526 (2026)

Jungmok Seo, who now leads the BLISS Lab in the School of Electrical and Electronic Engineering at Yonsei University, is the corresponding author of this paper.

DOI: 10.1038/s41467-026-72068-6 ↗

Why this research matters

A double-network hydrogel uses quinone-mediated bonding to adhere to wet tissue. The study tests adhesion on porcine skin and evaluates patch and injectable applications in animal bleeding and incision models.

Hydrogels for wet-tissue adhesion and hemostasis

Wet tissue is irregular, moves under load and carries a layer of water. STAT combines a hydrated gel with tissue-binding chemistry to maintain contact in those conditions. Following the tissue's shape and holding that contact under force are related but separate requirements.

How the study approaches it

The double network combines quinone-mediated tissue binding with tannic-acid-associated hemostatic functions. Conformability and adhesion are designed together. Although a separate activation step is not needed when applying the gel, oxidation is still controlled during preparation.

What was tested

Adhesion measurements depend on tissue, hydration, contact conditions and the direction of loading. Shear stress and peel energy measure different aspects of a bond. Animal bleeding tests add evidence about hemostasis but cannot substitute for adhesion tests on every tissue.

What the study found

Before oxidation, the gel attached to porcine skin but was easy to remove; oxidized STAT resisted removal. Both shear and 180-degree peel tests showed increased adhesion, linking catechol-to-quinone conversion with stronger bonding. These observations apply to the tested tissue and conditions, without establishing results for all tissues or human surgery.

What remains to be answered

Comparisons need matched tissue, contact time, loading and gel state. Storage, repeated deformation and longer-term tissue response require additional evidence. The summary draws on selected main-text explanations; supplements were not fully reviewed and no clinical protocol is proposed.

How it connects to other research

The related dry double-sided tape absorbs water at the interface before forming tissue bonds. STAT instead begins as a hydrated gel. This is a useful comparison of bonding mechanisms, but the external abstract cannot rank adhesion or hemostasis across different tissues and test geometries.
