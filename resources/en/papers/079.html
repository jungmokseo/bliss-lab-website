Hydrogel Ink for 3D Printing with High and Widely Tunable Mechanical Properties via the Salting‐Out Effect

Kun Woo Ko, Taehoon Lee, Jae-Hak Jeong, Yeontaek Lee, Ikjin Kim, Jungmok Seo, Yong-Hwa Park, Jin-Oh Kim*, Steve Park*

Advanced Materials Technologies, 9(10), 2400157 (2024)

Jungmok Seo, who now leads the BLISS Lab in the School of Electrical and Electronic Engineering at Yonsei University, is a co-author of this paper.

DOI: 10.1002/admt.202400157 ↗

Why this research matters

Salting-out tunes hydrogel-ink gelation and stiffness to combine printability with tissue-like mechanical properties. The study demonstrates layered vessel models for suturing and electrocautery training.

What the study did

Salting-out adjusts when the ink forms a gel, enabling direct-ink writing of hydrogels with different stiffnesses.

The study addresses the balance between printability and final mechanics, using layered vessel models for suturing and electrocautery training as examples.

What remains to be answered

A similar modulus does not reproduce every feature of living vessels, including directional mechanics, fatigue or thrombosis. Training-model results do not establish implant performance.

What to compare

Match salt concentration, temperature, hydration and print direction. Tensile modulus and a tube's deformation under pressure describe different mechanical responses.

Related research

Highly stretchable and tough hydrogels

Sun and colleagues connect network structure with fracture resistance and energy dissipation. The present study asks how to make a desired mechanical response printable.

Fracture energy, modulus and print fidelity are different measures. Toughness reported in an abstract is not a benchmark for printing performance.
