Lubricant‐Infused Polymeric Interfaces: A Stretchable and Anti‐Fouling Surface for Implantable Biomaterials

Tae Young Kim, Soohwan An, Young Kim, Seung Yeop Han, Jeuhee Lee, Kijun Park, Sangin Kim, Jae Park, Soo A Kim, Justin J. Chung, Seung-Woo Cho, Jungmok Seo*

Advanced Functional Materials, 34(14), 2312740 (2024)

Jungmok Seo, who now leads the BLISS Lab in the School of Electrical and Electronic Engineering at Yonsei University, is the corresponding author of this paper.

DOI: 10.1002/adfm.202312740 ↗

Why this research matters

A stretchable adhesive layer, fluoropolymer and lubricant form a surface designed to resist fouling as a biomaterial deforms. The study compares coating cracks and liquid repellency under repeated stretching.

The problem

An antifouling coating has to remain intact when its substrate stretches. Cracks can expose the underlying surface and interrupt lubrication, so its initial repellency may not describe performance during movement. L-VIP addresses this link between stretchability and a continuous lubricated interface.

How the study approaches it

L-VIP combines a stretchable adhesive, a fluoropolymer and a lubricant. The adhesive attaches the coating to the substrate; the fluoropolymer and lubricant form the exposed interface. Attachment, crack resistance and fouling resistance are therefore connected properties that need separate tests.

What was tested

Blood droplets were compared on stretched bare and L-VIP-coated Ecoflex, while repeated-stretch tests compared cracking with conventional lubricant-infused coatings. Microscopy helped relate structure to droplet behavior. Subcutaneous PVA implantation and electrical-insulation tests addressed tissue response and insulation separately.

What the study found

Under the tested cycling conditions, L-VIP retained repellency without the cracks seen in conventional coatings. Beyond its stretchability limit, defects appeared and wetting changed. Together, the structural and wetting observations show why performance during movement matters as well as initial surface repellency.

What remains to be answered

Retrieved implants were immersed in PFPE lubricant again before antifouling and contact-angle tests. Those results describe function after reconditioning, not uninterrupted retention of the original lubricant. Short-term tissue response, lubricant depletion and persistence of fluorinated liquid remain separate questions; maintenance-free implantation is not established.

Surface tests on retrieved implants were performed after re-infusing PFPE lubricant. They do not by themselves establish retention of the original lubricant throughout implantation.

How it connects to other research

The SLIPS abstract describes retaining liquid in a structured surface. L-VIP extends this design question to attachment and cracking on stretching substrates. Pressure stability and resistance to stretch-induced cracks test different aspects of a lubricated interface and cannot be compared as a single performance number.
