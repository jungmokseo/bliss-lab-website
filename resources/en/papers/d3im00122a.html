Liquid-based electronic materials for bioelectronics: current trends and challenges

Kijun Park, Sangwoo Park, Yejin Jo, Soo A Kim, Tae Young Kim, Sangwon Kim, Jungmok Seo*

Industrial Chemistry & Materials, 2(3), 361-377 (2024)

Jungmok Seo, who now leads the BLISS Lab in the School of Electrical and Electronic Engineering at Yonsei University, is the corresponding author of this paper.

DOI: 10.1039/d3im00122a ↗

Why this research matters

This review examines how liquid-based materials can provide conduction, moisture protection and tissue adhesion in bioelectronics. It considers how layers and components with different functions can work together, alongside the limits of each design strategy.

The problem

A deformable conductor is only one part of a bioelectronic device. It also needs protection from water and reliable tissue contact. This review brings conduction, encapsulation and adhesion together to show how layers with different functions work in an assembled device.

How the study approaches it

The review compares the stiffness and bulk of mechanical seals with the voids and cracks that can occur in polymer films. It then considers lubricant-infused interfaces as a compliant way to manage defects and water-entry paths. This is a barrier-design strategy; its effectiveness must still be established for each material and device.

What was tested

A modulus close to that of tissue does not ensure adhesion. High water content and low stiffness can weaken a hydrogel's integrity, motivating combinations of crosslinking and tissue-binding sites. Mechanical matching and interfacial bonding therefore need separate evaluation, alongside conduction and moisture protection.

What the study found

Solid components still supply functions and stability that fully soft systems cannot always provide. The review's integration challenge is to connect those components through compliant materials while managing water exposure and mechanical mismatch. Fully soft systems remain a future direction.

What remains to be answered

The review cannot rank materials or device lifetimes without examining the studies behind its tables. Maximum conductivity and strain may come from different specimens and loading histories. Its useful contribution is explaining which layer and failure mode each strategy addresses.

How it connects to other research

Yuk and colleagues' PEDOT:PSS printing study provides a concrete example of making conductive structures together with insulation. It helps connect the review's material categories to a device architecture. The results do not establish performance for liquid metals, ionic liquids or encapsulants as a whole.
