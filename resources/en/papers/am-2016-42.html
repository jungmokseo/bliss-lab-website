A facile method for the selective decoration of graphene defects based on a galvanic displacement reaction

Juree Hong, Jae-Bok Lee, Sanggeun Lee, Jungmok Seo, Hyunsoo Lee, Jeong Young Park, Jong-Hyun Ahn, Tae Il Seo, Taeyoon Lee*, Han-Bo-Ram Lee*

NPG Asia Materials, 8(4), e262 (2016)

Jungmok Seo, who now leads the BLISS Lab in the School of Electrical and Electronic Engineering at Yonsei University, is a co-author of this paper.

DOI: 10.1038/am.2016.42 ↗

Overview

This study, co-authored by Jungmok Seo (then at the Nanobio Device Laboratory, School of Electrical and Electronic Engineering, Yonsei University), who now leads the BLISS Lab at Yonsei University, used a wet-chemistry galvanic displacement reaction to deposit palladium nanoparticles selectively on defects in chemical vapor deposition (CVD) graphene. The sheet resistance decreased from approximately 1 kΩ/sq to 173 Ω/sq at 91.3% transmittance, and a transparent heater made from palladium-decorated graphene with 154 Ω/sq reached 94.4 °C at 12 V, compared with 60.6 °C for pristine graphene.

Background

CVD graphene can be produced at industrial scale, but the growth process leaves grain boundaries, wrinkles and cracks that raise the sheet resistance of single-layer graphene above 1 kΩ/sq, far from the theoretical value of approximately 30 Ω/sq. Earlier routes to improve transport, such as enlarging grains, bridging grains with metal nanowires or selective atomic layer deposition, require sophisticated treatments, high-cost equipment and long processing times. A simpler solution-based method that filled cracks with metal nanoparticles did not address grain boundaries or wrinkles.

Design and approach

Graphene grown on copper foil is immersed in an aqueous PdCl2 solution while still on the foil. In this graphene-buffered galvanic displacement reaction, the copper underneath acts as the anode and dissolves, and the graphene acts as the cathode that supplies electrons for palladium reduction. Defect sites are chemically less stable than the basal plane, so at low PdCl2 concentrations palladium nucleates preferentially at grain boundaries, wrinkles, regions above copper fatigue cracks and exposed edges of multilayer islands. The deposition pattern therefore serves both to decorate and to visualize the defects.

How it was evaluated

The authors varied PdCl2 concentration between 0.01 and 5 mM and reaction time, and examined the deposits by field-emission scanning electron microscopy, spherical aberration-corrected scanning transmission electron microscopy with selected-area diffraction, friction force microscopy and Raman spectroscopy. Sheet resistance was measured by the four-probe method together with optical transmittance. Transparent heaters (20 mm × 20 mm) made from pristine and palladium-decorated graphene were driven at a constant 12 V and monitored with an infrared camera, including under bending with a 10 mm curvature.

Key results

Deposition selectivity depended on concentration. A 5 mM solution for 5 min produced a continuous palladium film, deposition above 0.5 mM was not selective, and selective deposition was observed only below 0.5 mM. The palladium nanoparticles averaged approximately 12 nm in size. Diffraction patterns on either side of the polygonal palladium lines showed different lattice orientations, identifying those lines as grain boundaries. Parallel palladium lines matched the shallow copper fatigue cracks, whose spacing is approximately 200 nm, and palladium lines inside grain-boundary-free single domains showed that wrinkles also act as nucleation sites. On stacked islands, palladium formed only along external edges and not along edges buried under another layer, which allowed the stacking configuration to be traced.

Raman spectra showed only minor changes in D, G and 2D peak intensities, indicating little damage to the lattice. After reaction in 0.1 mM PdCl2 for 10 min, the G peak shifted from 1580 to 1595 cm−1 and the 2D peak from 2680 to 2688 cm−1, which the authors assigned to hole doping by palladium. Under the same condition, the nanoparticles connected into nanowire-like lines and the sheet resistance reached 173 Ω/sq, a decrease of approximately 83%. Transmittance fell from approximately 97% for pristine graphene to 91.3%. For comparison, the authors cited approximately 125 Ω/sq at 97.4% transmittance reported elsewhere for HNO3-doped graphene.

In the heater test at 12 V, the steady-state temperature increased as sheet resistance decreased. A palladium-decorated heater with 154 Ω/sq reached 94.4 °C, whereas the pristine graphene heater reached 60.6 °C. During bending with a 10 mm curvature, the maximum temperature dropped early in the test to 85.6 °C, a decrease of approximately 9%, and then remained stable over continued bending; the authors attributed the drop to detachment of some nanoparticles and increased contact resistance. A test with AgNO3 also produced selective silver deposition at defect sites.

Open questions

The authors noted that palladium patterns alone do not easily distinguish grain boundaries from wrinkles, that the exact stacking configuration of multilayer islands could not be confirmed by friction force microscopy, and that the relative contributions of chemical doping and added conduction paths to the lower sheet resistance could not be separated with their setup.

How it connects to other research

Selective atomic layer deposition at graphene line defects places metal on defects from the vapor phase, whereas this study reaches the same defect types from solution by using the copper growth substrate as the electron source.
