A Mechanically Resilient and Tissue‐Conformable Hydrogel with Hemostatic and Antibacterial Capabilities for Wound Care

Jae Park†, Tae Young Kim†, Yeonju Kim, Soohwan An, Kyeong Seok Kim, Minkyong Kang, Soo A Kim, Jayoung Kim, Joonseok Lee, Seung-Woo Cho, Jungmok Seo*

Advanced Science, 10(30), 2303651 (2023)

Jungmok Seo, who now leads the BLISS Lab in the School of Electrical and Electronic Engineering at Yonsei University, is the corresponding author of this paper.

DOI: 10.1002/advs.202303651 ↗

Why this research matters

This wound-care hydrogel combines tannic acid with a polymer network to maintain contact with moving, wet tissue. The study separately evaluates porcine-skin adhesion, antibacterial activity in culture and acute hemostasis in a mouse liver-injury model.

The problem

A dressing on a wet, moving wound must stay attached while accommodating tissue deformation. If it cannot move with the tissue, stress can build up in the patch or at its edges. This study combines compliant, energy-dissipating mechanics with hemostatic and antibacterial functions.

How the study approaches it

Reversible interactions in the tannic-acid/PVA/PAA network let bonds disengage under load and form again, helping the gel dissipate energy and recover. Surface activation supports tissue adhesion separately. Recovery within the gel and strong attachment to wet tissue therefore require different evidence.

What was tested

Mechanical tests, porcine-skin contact, bacterial cultures and mouse experiments assess different functions. Skin tests examine contact during movement; liver injury tests examine acute bleeding; subcutaneous implantation examines tissue response. Bacterial-culture activity does not establish treatment of an infected wound, and these tests should not be combined into a general healing claim.

What the study found

The patch reduced blood loss relative to untreated wounds and a PVA control in the mouse liver-injury model. The PVA control provides a comparison with a simpler polymer system. The result supports acute hemostasis in this model, while leaving the individual contributions of adhesion, conformity and biological interactions unresolved.

What remains to be answered

Acute bleeding control and short-term implantation do not establish long-term wound healing or regeneration. Comparisons need matched activation, contact pressure and injury conditions. Antibacterial activity in culture also needs to be distinguished from healing an infected wound.

How it connects to other research

Li and colleagues' abstract separates tissue bonding from energy dissipation within an adhesive. That distinction helps explain the TA-containing patch's design. Comparing the two requires matched tissue-adhesion tests and, separately, matched bleeding models; adhesion energy and hemostasis time measure different outcomes.
