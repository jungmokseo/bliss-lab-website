Encapsulation Strategies for OLEDs: From Conventional Devices to Flexible and Biointegrated Applications

Jangho Kim†, Sangwoo Park, Tae Young Kim, Yejin Jo, Kijun Park*, Jungmok Seo*

Advanced Electronic Materials, 11(19), e00475 (2025)

Jungmok Seo, who now leads the BLISS Lab in the School of Electrical and Electronic Engineering at Yonsei University, is the corresponding author of this paper.

DOI: 10.1002/aelm.202500475 ↗

Why this research matters

This review examines encapsulation strategies that protect flexible OLEDs from moisture and oxygen while accommodating bending. It connects film-barrier properties, deformation-induced defects and operating-device degradation across conventional displays and biointegrated applications.

The problem

Flexible encapsulation must keep out moisture while withstanding repeated movement. Inorganic films can crack, polymers can transmit moisture, and interfaces can contain defects. The review connects these different failure routes to the protection a device needs.

How the study approaches it

Combining barrier layers with compliant layers does not automatically remove interfacial defects. Bioelectronic applications also require chemical stability and appropriate tissue interaction. Low permeability in an isolated film is therefore only one part of the evidence for an integrated device.

What was tested

Water-vapor tests use different principles and sensitivities, including cup, instrument-based, impedance and calcium methods. Temperature, humidity, thickness and detection conditions all affect comparison. Measuring barrier performance after bending is needed to connect a film's deformation history to its protective function.

What the study found

Changes in brightness, voltage and dark spots reveal device degradation, while permeability tests characterize the film. A single mechanical test also cannot represent every use condition. The review links evidence from an intact film, a deformed film and an operating device in separate stages.

What remains to be answered

Practical questions include low-temperature processing, defect control over large areas and manufacturing reproducibility. Accelerated aging requires a suitable model of failure before it can predict use lifetime. The cited numerical studies were not individually reviewed, so the summary does not identify one universally best barrier.

How it connects to other research

Ultraflexible organic photonic skin provides a device-level example of thin encapsulation and conformal contact. It helps connect film properties to optoelectronic operation. Its abstract does not establish a lifetime or safety benchmark for all implantable bioelectronics.
